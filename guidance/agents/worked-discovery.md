# Worked discovery: an exhibition invitation

**Authored illustration.** The exhibition, source cards and later visitor contribution are teaching material. The written products show a connected construction; visitor reception has not been observed.

## The brief and available material

A client wants an optional invitation that helps visitors develop relationships among three exhibition objects. Keep the captions accurate within the supplied record, leave original objects unmoved and unaltered, and allow participation without writing. Reproductions may be arranged.

| Object | Supplied relationship | Open historical detail |
|---|---|---|
| Cup | Its handle has a visible repair. | Who repaired it or when |
| Ticket | A date is printed on it. | Who used it or whether it was used |
| Notebook | It contains a table of measurements. | The measurements’ purpose |

A common owner or historical episode would require additional support. The available material already permits constructing an activity around visible features, questions and possible connections.

The task contains different degrees of settlement:

| Participation | How it enters the work |
|---|---|
| Accurate captions, unchanged originals, nonwritten participation | Fixed duties supplied by the brief |
| Source cards, permission to arrange reproductions, current interpretive competence | Available inputs and capacities |
| A useful invitation and receiving practice | Relationships the agent can construct now |
| What an actual visitor understands or pursues | A return still needed for a claim about visitor use |

There is no need to rediscover the fixed duties or invent a shared history before developing the invitation. Discovery remains open where the contribution needs it.

## Project what the invitation could enable

**Hypothesis:** an invitation can help visitors construct a question from relationships among the objects.

Follow that possibility into its consequences. Choosing a relationship gives attention a direction. Connecting it to a feature or caption gives further inquiry a starting point. Asking what would help develop it can give the next source encounter a purpose. The activity therefore needs to connect a proposed relationship, its present support and a possible continuation.

Follow the relationship further. If the offered question reaches someone who can develop it, the invitation becomes an entrance to a continuing inquiry. A useful next source or arrangement could then change what the visitor can compare. Retaining that contrast could help staff receive a different question later. The invitation, receiving practice and reusable example can develop together.

Those implications also shape the medium. A visitor should be able to participate by pointing, speaking or looking, as the brief allows. A prompt seeking one intended answer would limit the relationships available for development; a request for a complete story could encourage unsupported historical attribution. The owed contribution is an accessible, source-faithful invitation to develop relationships. The stronger projection concerns how the inquiry could continue through a receiving practice and further sources.

## Let the partial artifact change the construction

The initial draft is:

> Choose two objects. Tell the story that connects them.

Reading it against the source cards exposes a problem: “the story” suggests an established connection. It also asks for a completed narrative before the visitor has developed a question. Repair the invitation itself:

> **Make a connection**
>
> Choose two objects. What relationship is worth exploring?
>
> Point to something in the objects or their captions that helps you begin.
>
> What would help you develop that relationship further?
>
> You may point, speak, sketch, or simply keep looking.

Add a support line:

> The captions state what the supplied records establish. A proposed connection can become a question, an interpretation or a story; it is not automatically a shared history of the objects.

The first hypothesis has now produced an invitation and a more specific question: how can the next receiving operation help develop whichever relationship a visitor offers?

## Make the same preparation serve different uses

The source cards and draft can be read together through several interests. Each has a particular contribution in the artifact:

| Interest | Contribution to inspect |
|---|---|
| Source stewardship | The source cards and support line distinguish established history from proposed connections. |
| Inquiry | The invitation connects a relationship to a feature and a possible next source. |
| Access | The response options allow nonwritten participation and continued looking. |
| Further development | The next receiver can work from the contribution that actually arrives. |

A problem found through one interest may call for a change elsewhere. For example, historical attribution could be improved by changing the activity or arranging reproductions, rather than adding more explanation to the captions. Shared reading helps locate the relationship that needs work.

## Prepare reception

Write a staff note that makes the next operation usable:

> Receive the visitor’s contribution before deciding what it means. Ask what in the objects or captions matters to their connection. If historical attribution is consequential, distinguish the supplied record from the proposed link. Develop a relevant next source, comparison or arrangement where available. Follow the contribution actually offered, including a new question or a decision to keep looking.

This extends the construction from a printed invitation to an invitation used with a receiving practice. The staff can help an unexpected relationship develop, and a later return can change the prompt, the material shown, or the activity.

## Carry a return into the next product

Continue the authored case with this visitor contribution:

> The cup has a repair and the notebook keeps measurements. Could we compare what each preserves, rather than assume they share a history?

The returned question changes the use. More historical research could still be valuable, but common ownership is unnecessary for this comparison. The source cards already establish a visible repair and recorded measurements. They do not establish the repair's purpose, the cup's present usability or the measurements' intended use.

Construct a comparison card for this inquiry:

> **Compare what remains available**
>
> Point to one visible feature in each object. What is kept here?
>
> What further use might that make possible? What would help you decide?
>
> Keep the visible feature, proposed use and unanswered question distinct. You may point, speak, sketch or keep looking.

Adapt the receiving note as well:

> Begin from what the visitor says is preserved. Ask which possible use matters. A question about an object's history can select a particular record or source. A comparison of possible functions can develop through the visible material and an explicit hypothesis. Choose the next contact from the question actually being pursued.

The return has entered two producers: the card that invites the next contribution and the staff practice that receives it. It also changes the useful comparison. The question is now whether the arrangement helps distinguish what is retained, what it could enable and what remains to be learned. Factual accuracy and access remain part of the original duty.

The broader possibility is a receiving practice that helps many kinds of visitor question develop. The particular preservation question supplies one worked contrast for that practice; it need not become every visitor's topic.

## Retain the useful relationship and select again

Keep the source cards, actual permissions, original invitation, scoped comparison card and receiving note. Retain the contrast that changed “the story” into an opening for inquiry, and the later return that selected a different next product. The reusable contribution joins a proposed relationship to its grounds and next possible contact. Another task can reconstruct it through a different medium or source.

If further work is warranted, compare another wording revision with a broader receiving practice for varied questions, a source encounter that could settle a consequential historical question, or an arrangement of reproductions that makes a useful comparison visible. Develop the promising direction far enough to see what it enables and what participation it needs. A completed commission may instead finish.

For actual use, receive what a participant develops, the material they use and what they want to pursue. Carry a consequential return into its next product. This illustration leaves complete written products and their constructive relationship available. Visitor efficacy remains unobserved; investigate it when a selected decision requires that evidence.
